Research

How does the way we live tune the cells that defend us?

I work where human physiology meets cellular immunology, with a focus on killer T cells (the immune cells that hunt down infected and cancerous cells) and on how to make them better medicines. Alongside the bench, I study the integrity of the scientific record itself.

Active questions

01
ActiveSalk Institute · 2025 –Patent application filed

How do the body's daily rhythms shape T cells, and can we use them to build better cell therapies?

Our immune system oscillates across the day, synchronised to the rotation of the Earth by cues such as light and food. Both the migration and the activity of T lymphocytes are under circadian control, and recent work suggests that even the time of day at which cancer immunotherapies are given can change how well they work.

Yet when we grow cells in the lab, including the T cells manufactured for adoptive cell therapies, we culture them in constant conditions. I'm exploring how reintroducing physiological rhythms changes T cell growth, differentiation and function, and how the timing of food intake affects T cell immunity against cancer. This work underpins a provisional patent application on adoptive T cell therapy.

Background: meal timing and immunity

Work done predominantly in nocturnal rodents has revealed that both the migration patterns and cellular activity of T lymphocytes oscillate under circadian control, and the importance of this regulation has been highlighted across a range of cancer types. Diet is a powerful and controllable circadian entrainment cue. In humans, time-restricted eating, where all daily calories are consumed within an 8 to 12 hour window, has been shown to improve health outcomes in patients with metabolic disease and in shift workers. In mice, time-restricted feeding (TRF) benefits liver metabolism and gene expression, and has beneficial effects in certain liver cancers and in Alzheimer's disease.

Yet the direct effect of TRF on the immune system had not been investigated. This project asks whether TRF can optimise rhythmicity and synchronicity across peripheral clocks, including those in T cells, and thereby enhance immunity, particularly anticancer immunity in immunotherapeutic settings.

02
ActiveMeta-scienceResearch integrity

How much of the scientific record can we trust, and how do we fix the incentives that distort it?

Many influential preclinical findings fail to replicate. I'm interested in how distortion enters the literature (through fraud, funding and publication bias, flexible statistics, and the way science is reported), and in what institutions could do differently.

In 2026 I co-authored a formal peer review in Theory and Society showing how a series of analytic choices in a published study of brain anatomy biased its results toward the authors' hypothesis. At the 2026 Avalon Exposition in Berkeley I gave a talk, “Science is largely fake”, on the biases that distort scientific truth and the prevalence of influential fake and fraudulent science, including unpublished replication data from cancer immunotherapy.

I write regularly about publishing, hiring, collaboration and the structure of academic labs on Fruit of Eden, and as a 2026 Roots of Progress Fellow.

How killer T cells kill

From 2020 to 2025 I did my PhD in the lab of Prof Gillian Griffiths at the Cambridge Institute for Medical Research, studying the cell biology of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) as they find, grip and destroy their targets.

03
Manuscript in revisionCDC42

What does a “brain” version of CDC42 do in killer T cells?

I discovered that CTLs express an unusual splice isoform of the protein CDC42 that was previously thought to be expressed only in the brain. It turns out to be an important regulator of how these cells deliver their killing machinery to cancer cells.

Read the abstract

The control of polarized secretion from cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) requires rapid and accurate delivery of secretory lysosomes to the immune synapse. Previous studies have shown that secretory lysosomes undergo dynein-directed transport towards the polarized centrosome. How this process is regulated is not known. We investigate CTLs from a human patient with a pathogenic C-terminal CDC42 mutation, but find that these CTLs retain full killing capacity. This mutation only affects the “ubiquitous” splice isoform of CDC42 (CDC42u). However, we find that CTLs also express the alternative “brain” splice isoform (CDC42b). The active pool of CDC42 is localized primarily on secretory lysosomes in CTLs, and inhibition of CDC42 activity or overexpression of constitutively inactive CDC42b impairs their polarised secretion. We find that CDC42 inhibition does not impact dynein-tether recruitment, but prevents dynein-mediated transport of lysosomes. These findings reveal a novel role for the brain isoform of CDC42 in regulating secretory lysosome polarization, critical for CTL-mediated killing.

WithCarsten Speckmann (University of Freiburg) · Doreen Cantrell (University of Dundee) PresentedLMB–Karolinska Student Symposium 2024
04
Published 2025Journal of Cell Science

Why does deleting an essential gene sometimes make T cells better?

When I deleted Cdc42 from CTLs using CRISPR/Cas9, their killing ability dropped, then rapidly recovered and even exceeded normal levels while the protein remained absent. Chemical inhibition told a different story. The cells had rewired their gene expression to compensate, a striking example of “transcriptional adaptation” and a reminder to study protein function from more than one angle.

Read the abstract

Cdc42 is a Rho family GTPase known for its central role in cell polarity and cytoskeletal regulation. To understand the role of Cdc42 in polarised secretion from cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) we used CRISPR/Cas9 gene deletion. Although Cdc42-deleted CTLs initially showed reduced cytotoxicity for up to 2 days after CRISPR-mediated deletion, full secretion and cytotoxicity was rapidly restored and even enhanced while CDC42 protein remained absent. In contrast, chemical inhibition of CDC42 using CASIN consistently decreased secretion in wild-type cells, but had no impact on Cdc42-deleted CTLs, confirming the specificity of this inhibitor. Comparative proteomics and transcriptomics of CTLs after Cdc42 deletion revealed transcriptional changes that could support improved T cell function, including compensation via other Rho GTPases. Targeting the promoter region of Cdc42 did not trigger transcriptional adaptation, consistent with a nonsense-mediated decay mechanism of genetic compensation. Our work highlights the importance of taking orthogonal approaches to study protein function and reveals the remarkable robustness of primary T cells to adapt to loss of an essential gene.

05
Published 2026Communications Biology

How does the actin cytoskeleton change shape as killer T cells push, pull and kill?

Using probes that recognise particular conformations of actin filaments, combined with traction force microscopy, I visualised the internal cytoskeleton and the external forces of migrating and killing CTLs at the same time. Changes in actin conformation come first; force follows.

Read the abstract

Cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) are cells of the adaptive immune system that are able to recognise and kill cancerous or virally infected target cells. The biochemical basis of CTL-mediated killing has been examined in great detail. Recent work has underscored the importance of physical forces in T cell function too, but how CTLs sense and exert force during migration and killing is not fully understood. Here, by expressing actin conformation probes based on the CH domain of utrophin, we directly visualize regions of altered F-actin conformation in primary CTLs during migration and killing. By combining these probes with traction force microscopy, we correlate external force with the internal cytoskeleton. We show that actin conformation is regulated upstream of force production, and that force exertion at the cytotoxic immune synapse temporally follows actin conformation dynamics. Our work offers insight into the relationship between cellular force production and F-actin conformation in important primary immune cells.
