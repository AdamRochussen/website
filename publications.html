Theory and Society · 55(5), 84

Flawed methods and overstated conclusions regarding ‘Brain Sex’: a peer review of Kurth et al. (2022)

Wright CM, Rochussen AM, Stagg SG

NewMeta-scienceCritique
DOI
Plain-language summary AI-generated

A 2022 study concluded that the brain anatomy of transgender women is shifted away from their biological sex and toward their gender identity. This peer review re-examines whether the evidence supports that claim. It finds that the headline result rests on a very small sample, weakly justified parametric statistics, one-tailed post hoc tests, no correction for multiple comparisons and little robustness testing. Sexual orientation, a potential confound in brain anatomy, was not properly accounted for even though the data to do so were available. Because each of these choices pushes towards smaller p-values, the authors conclude that the study's evidence does not justify its headline conclusion.

Communications Biology · 9, 1256

Actin conformation dynamics precede force generation in cytotoxic T lymphocytes

Rochussen AM, Kebabci AO, Winkel AK, Franze K, Fletcher DA, Griffiths GM, Lippert AH

NewFirst authorImmunology
DOI bioRxiv preprint
Plain-language summary AI-generated

Killer T cells exert physical forces as they crawl through tissue and attack their targets, but how their internal skeleton produces those forces has been unclear. This study used fluorescent probes that recognise particular shapes (conformations) of actin filaments, alongside traction force microscopy to measure the forces the cells exert on their surroundings, to watch both at once in living T cells. Changes in actin conformation happen first, and force generation follows, including at the immune synapse where the T cell kills. This suggests that the shape of actin filaments is regulated upstream of force production.

Science Immunology · 10(111), eadt5909

Nuclear polarization to the immune synapse facilitates an early transcriptional burst

Asano Y, Ma CY, Limbäck-Stokin MM, Rochussen AM, Stinchcombe JC, Griffiths GM

Immunology
DOI
Plain-language summary AI-generated

When a killer T cell recognises a target, it rapidly reorganises itself toward the point of contact, the immune synapse. This study found that the nucleus itself moves to and touches the synapse, deforming as it goes, and gets there before the centrosome docks. As the nucleus moves, the transcription factors NFAT and NF-κB accumulate inside it. Blocking or deleting the motor protein myosin IIA prevented both events. The authors propose that moving the nucleus to the synapse helps trigger an early burst of gene expression, so that freshly made cytokines can be delivered straight to the target.

Journal of Cell Science · 138(15), jcs263826

Transcriptional adaptation after deletion of Cdc42 in primary T cells

Rochussen AM, Ma CY, Griffiths GM

First authorImmunology
DOI Editor's summary
Plain-language summary AI-generated

CDC42 is thought to be essential for cell polarity, yet when it was deleted from killer T cells using CRISPR, their killing ability dropped only briefly. Within days it recovered and even exceeded normal levels, while the protein stayed absent. A chemical inhibitor of CDC42, by contrast, consistently reduced secretion in normal cells and had no effect on the knockouts. Proteomics and transcriptomics showed that the knockout cells had rewired their gene expression, including compensating through other Rho GTPases, consistent with a nonsense-mediated decay mechanism of “transcriptional adaptation”. The lesson is that genetic knockouts and acute inhibition can tell very different stories, so it pays to use more than one approach.

Journal of Experimental Medicine · 222(1), e20220979

Dominant negative variants in ITPR3 impair T cell Ca2+ dynamics causing combined immunodeficiency

Blanco E, Camps C, Bahal S, Kerai MD, Ferla MP, Rochussen AM, Handel AE, … Griffiths GM, Thrasher AJ, Dhalla F, Kreins AY (41 authors)

ImmunologyClinical
DOI
Plain-language summary AI-generated

Calcium signalling is essential for switching T cells on. This study describes five unrelated patients with new (de novo) mutations in ITPR3, a gene encoding part of a calcium channel in the cell's internal calcium store, the endoplasmic reticulum. The patients had combined immunodeficiency, with low T cell numbers, defects in T cell development in the thymus and poor activation of the T cells they did have, alongside problems in other organ systems. The mutant protein doesn't simply reduce the amount of working channel. It actively interferes with it, draining internal calcium stores and blunting the calcium entry T cells need to respond.

Open Biology · 14(2), 230456

NFIL3 contributes to cytotoxic T lymphocyte-mediated killing

Douanne T, Strege K, Del Castillo Velasco-Herrera M, Rochussen AM, Adams DJ, Griffiths GM

Immunology
DOI
Plain-language summary AI-generated

To find new regulators of how killer T cells kill, this study compared gene expression in resting and activated CD8+ T cells, then ran a targeted CRISPR screen on the genes switched on during activation. It identified NFIL3, a transcription factor already known to act in several other immune cell types. Removing NFIL3 markedly reduced killing, even though early signalling, immune synapse formation and granule release were all intact. The reason: NFIL3 controls production of the cytolytic proteins and effector cytokines that do the damage.

Current Opinion in Immunology · 82, 102309

Imaging the T-cell receptor: new approaches, new insights

Rochussen AM, Lippert AH, Griffiths GM

First authorReviewImmunology
DOI
Plain-language summary AI-generated

T cells recognise threats through the T-cell receptor (TCR). How TCRs are arranged on the surface of a resting T cell matters for explaining how a single recognition event can switch the cell on so quickly, yet recent imaging studies disagree about that arrangement. This review compares the conflicting results from techniques including total internal reflection and single-molecule localisation microscopy, examines the biases built into each approach, and discusses evidence that the surfaces cells are imaged on can themselves change T cell activation.

2022Sep

Communications Biology · 5, 1010

Targeting non-coding RNA family members with artificial endonuclease XNAzymes

Donde MJ, Rochussen AM, Kapoor S, Taylor AI

RNA therapeutics
DOI
Plain-language summary AI-generated

Non-coding RNAs are promising drug targets, but closely related members of the same RNA family are hard to hit selectively. This study engineered synthetic catalytic oligonucleotides, called XNAzymes, made from an artificial nucleic acid (FANA). They specifically or preferentially cut individual family members under near-physiological conditions, including cancer-associated microRNAs from the miR-17~92 cluster and the Y RNA hY5. Three anti-microRNA XNAzymes were also self-assembled into a stable nanostructure targeting miR-17, miR-20a and miR-21, pointing towards precise RNA knockdown with fewer off-target effects.
